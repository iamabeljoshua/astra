# sky-abel

Prompt v1. Level out_of_school, interests music, goal curiosity.

Student played by a cheap model: You are an adult who left school years ago. You never learned that sunlight is a mix of colours, and you have no idea what scattering or wavelength mean. You want to really get it. If the tutor uses a word you have not met, you say so, or you say it lost you. After a few turns, if you still feel lost, say: explain like i am 5.

**Student:** why is the sky blue?

**Tutor** (45 words, longest stretch 20, first text 2221 ms):
> Sunlight looks white, but it's really every color mixed together.
>
> When it hits air, the tiny gas molecules scatter blue light much more than red.
>
> So blue gets bounced all over the sky, and that's what reaches your eyes. Does that make sense so far?

Board: `text White light = all colors mixed`

**Student:** i kind of follow the white light being all colors mixed. but "scatter" and "molecules" lost me a bit. what does scatter mean here?

**Tutor** (46 words, longest stretch 28, first text 2109 ms):
> Fair enough. Scatter just means bounced in all different directions.
>
> Think of light as a stream of little pulses heading straight at you. When one hits a speck of air, it gets knocked sideways instead of carrying on.
>
> Blue gets knocked around much more than red.

Board: `focus`

**Student:** ok so blue gets knocked sideways more. but why does blue get knocked more than red? i don't get that bit.

**Tutor** (56 words, longest stretch 28, first text 1272 ms):
> Here's the short version. Blue light has shorter waves than red.
>
> The air molecules are tiny, so they push around short waves much more easily than long ones.
>
> I'm not sure I can give you the full why without some physics math. The name for it is Rayleigh scattering, if you want to look it up.

Board: `focus`, `writeMath \text{blue: short waves}`

**Student:** ok so

**Tutor** (4 words, longest stretch 4, first text 1679 ms):
> Go ahead, I'm listening.

Board: `focus`

**Student:** so blue is the short wave one, and the air bits push those around more. but i still don't really get why short waves get pushed more. explain like i am 5.

**Tutor** (40 words, longest stretch 17, first text 1324 ms):
> Okay, picture a tiny pebble sitting in a pond.
>
> Long, lazy waves just roll right past it. Quick, choppy ripples slap into it and splash everywhere.
>
> Blue light is the choppy ripples. That's the rough idea, and it's not perfect.

Board: `focus`

**Student:** ok so the choppy ones hit the pebble and splash, and the long lazy ones just roll past. so blue is the splashy one. that kinda makes sense, i think.

**Tutor** (39 words, longest stretch 26, first text 1377 ms):
> Yep, you've got it. Blue splashes everywhere, red mostly rolls straight on through.
>
> That's also why sunsets look red. The light travels through so much air that most of the blue has splashed away. Only the red is left.

Board: `focus`

**Student:** oh wait so at sunset the blue has all splashed off already, so only red gets to my eyes? that's kinda neat. is that right?

**Tutor** (49 words, longest stretch 28, first text 1517 ms):
> Yes, that's right. At sunset the light slants through a lot more air, so most of the blue gets scattered off to the sides before it reaches you.
>
> What's left is the reds and oranges. And that blue isn't gone, it's lighting up the sky for people somewhere else.

Board: `focus`

**Student:** wait, so the blue that splashed off at my sunset is lighting up the sky for someone else right now? that's wild. so it's always splashing somewhere, just not always toward me?

**Tutor** (43 words, longest stretch 24, first text 1272 ms):
> Exactly. Sunlight is always hitting the daytime side of Earth, and the blue is always getting splashed around somewhere.
>
> So the sky is blue wherever the sun is up, and your sunset is just the same blue being sent off toward someone else.
