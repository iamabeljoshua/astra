# sky-middle

Prompt v2. Level middle_school, interests football, goal class.

Student played by a cheap model: You are 13. You have seen rainbows but do not know why they happen. You think the sky is blue because it reflects the sea. You answer honestly and briefly, and you say huh when you do not follow.

**Student:** why is the sky blue

**Tutor** (12 words, longest stretch 12, first text 1737 ms):
> Ooh, okay. Sunlight looks white, but have you ever seen a rainbow?

**Student:** yeah, after rain sometimes. but i don't really know why they happen.

**Tutor** (19 words, longest stretch 19, first text 1111 ms):
> That's fine, here's all you need. Sunlight is secretly a mix of colors, and rain drops split them apart.

**Student:** huh, so the drops act like tiny prisms? i didn't know sunlight had colors inside it.

**Tutor** (20 words, longest stretch 20, first text 1402 ms):
> Yep, tiny prisms is exactly right. Now, which color do you think gets bounced around the most by the air?

**Student:** huh, um... i don't know. red? i think i'd guess red since it's the brightest.

**Tutor** (25 words, longest stretch 25, first text 1682 ms):
> Not red, it's actually the opposite end. Blue gets knocked around the most, because its waves are short and choppy, so air bounces it easily.

**Student:** oh, so blue gets bounced everywhere, that's why the sky looks blue? i thought it was from the sea though.

**Tutor** (29 words, longest stretch 29, first text 1789 ms):
> You've got it, blue bounces everywhere, so it reaches your eyes from every direction. The sea idea is backwards: the sea looks blue partly because it reflects the sky.

**Student:** oh wow, so the sea is blue because it copies the sky? that's kind of backwards from what i thought.

**Tutor** (21 words, longest stretch 21, first text 1952 ms):
> Partly, yes. Water also soaks up red light itself, so deep water ends up looking blue even without the sky's help.

**Student:** huh, so the water eats the red and keeps the blue? that's kind of wild. so is it the same thing that makes rainbows?

**Tutor** (22 words, longest stretch 22, first text 1530 ms):
> Different trick, same ingredient: sunlight's hidden colors. Rainbows come from drops bending each color a slightly different amount, not from bouncing around.

**Student:** oh, so the drops bend each color by a different amount, and that spreads them out? so the bending is different from the bouncing thing?

**Tutor** (28 words, longest stretch 28, first text 1544 ms):
> Exactly. Bending sorts colors into a neat stripe, bouncing scatters one color everywhere. So when the sun is low at sunset, what color do you think is left?
